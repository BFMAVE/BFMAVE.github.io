Brecht F. Verbeken*

I am an economist (PhD) and mathematician (MSc) by training, and I think of myself as a data scientist. My interests centre on economics and AI, with a lasting passion for mathematics.

I particularly enjoy bringing people with different specialities together and the cross-fertilisation of ideas that comes from interdisciplinary work. I often help set the research agenda and lead collaborations, as I have done since the early years of my PhD.

I am a postdoctoral researcher and guest professor at the Vrije Universiteit Brussel. More recently, I have become particularly interested in the economic impact of AI, especially how it changes scientific discovery and innovation. My work spans economic forecasting, model evaluation, and AI in mathematics and science.

Current research

Economics

Regarding my interests and work:

“An economist who is only an economist cannot even be a good economist.”

F. A. Hayek, interview with Leo Rosten (1978)

More recently, my interests have turned to the economic effects of AI, particularly its effects on scientific discovery and innovation. This includes how AI changes the pace and direction of research, which questions receive attention, and how verification costs affect the value of automation.

Colleagues at VUB, led by Andres Algaba, are exploring this line of work through The Rate and Direction of Automated Science, a project selected for OpenAI’s Economic Research Exchange.

Related work: LLM-generated research methods and scientific literature and citation practices.

My economics work also includes economic forecasting, decision-making under uncertainty, public investment and transport accessibility, and ergodicity economics with Arne Vanhoyweghen and colleagues.

I have also served as an examiner, supervisor or co-supervisor for master’s theses spanning a wide range of economics and business topics.

Mathematics

Mathematics is a lasting passion. My main mathematical research line concerns Markov chains and stochastic matrices, particularly their eigenvalues and spectral regions. It also connects to my work on semi-Markov models and applications in manpower planning.

I occasionally return to algebra, closer to my master’s thesis on units in integral group rings: group rings, the Prime Graph Question, and braces. More recently, a paper on Ramsey graphs has provided another setting in which to explore AI-assisted mathematical research.

A personal project close to my heart is an illustrated exploration of the Karpelevič theorem, developed with Vincent Ginis. It brings together a proof, geometric explanations and numerical illustrations of the possible eigenvalues of stochastic matrices. I see this kind of exposition as a service to the mathematical community—work that, in my view, is too often undervalued in science.
